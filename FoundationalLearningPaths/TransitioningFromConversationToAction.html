Transitioning From Conversation to Action

Turn an understanding of the student’s challenge into purposeful instruction and active participation.

Estimated duration2.5 hours over a 2-week period

Learning objectives

Select a study strategy that addresses a student’s stated challenge and explain the connection between the strategy and their challenge.
Introduce a strategy using specific language that explains its purpose and invites participation.
Distinguish coaching from advice-giving and personal storytelling, directing the session toward student participation.
Use resources as part of an active coaching experience by supporting navigation, reflection, and application.

Step 2

Coaching through a resource conversation

🔒 Attend the workshop and confirm your attendance above to open this reading.

Resource conversations can be valuable. The right resource can help a student access support, clarify a confusing concept, or find a useful way to work with their course material. The value comes from helping the student understand and use the resource in relation to their own challenge.

Keep the student actively involved.

Simply showing a handful of resources and explaining each one does not meet the expectations for a coaching interaction. Invite the student to navigate, reflect, evaluate, and apply what they find.

1. Let the student’s challenge guide the selection

During Frame the Session, ask enough questions to understand what the student wants to accomplish, what is getting in the way, what they have already tried, and what would make today’s appointment useful. Clarify vague challenges before recommending a resource.

Questions to establish the need
  • “What part of this course is giving you the most difficulty?”
  • “What happens when you try to work through that material?”
  • “What have you already tried, and how has it worked for you?”
  • “What would you like to be able to do by the end of our appointment?”

If the student says, “I need more resources,” explore what they hope another resource will help them do. A student struggling to understand an explanation has a different need from a student who cannot locate practice opportunities.

2. Choose one or two resources that fit

Show only one or two resources that align with the challenge. Explain the connection in specific language. Give the student room to evaluate whether the resource fits their needs rather than assuming it will work because you found it helpful.

“You mentioned that the written explanation is difficult to follow and you’d like to see an example. This course video walks through an example of that topic. Is it okay with you if we open that section together and see how useful it is for your question?”

Avoid turning the appointment into a tour of every available option. Explore a targeted resource in enough depth for the student to decide how they will use it.

3. Put navigation in the student’s hands

When you show where to find a resource, ask the student to follow along on their own device. Even better, invite them to share their screen while you talk them through the clicks. This lets you see where they need support and gives them experience accessing the resource themselves.

Give the student time to navigate. If they get stuck, offer a prompt and let them make the next click. When sharing a screen, they can share only the course window they need.

4. Explore the resource through coaching questions

Move beyond describing the resource’s features. Ask the student to examine how it connects to their challenge, what value it adds, and what it leaves unanswered. Choose questions that fit the moment and allow time for a thoughtful response.

Fit and value

  • “How does this connect to what you wanted help with today?”
  • “Which part looks most useful to you, and why?”
  • “What does this add to the material you have already used?”
  • “How does this explanation compare with the one in the textbook?”

Gaps and application

  • “What is still unclear or missing for you?”
  • “Where might you need another example or a different kind of support?”
  • “How could you use this with the section you are working on?”
  • “How will you tell whether using it is helping?”

Listen to the answers and let them shape the next step. If the resource does not address the challenge, acknowledge that and reconsider the fit together. You do not need to persuade the student to use it.

5. Give the student something meaningful to do

Build a small opportunity for application into the appointment. The student might locate the relevant section of a study guide, open a specific example, examine a practice question, or review a short portion of a course video and identify what it clarifies. The task should address their stated need.

A resource conversation does not always require a separate study-strategy demonstration. It still requires student participation. Watching you click through pages while you explain everything gives the student little opportunity to practice accessing or evaluating the resource.

“Let’s open the part that addresses the concept you mentioned. Take a moment to look through the example. What does it help you understand? What question do you still have?”

What this can sound like

Student: “I really need a study guide for this course. I need something that tells me what I’m supposed to be focusing on.”

Coach: “I completely understand. Courses can feel never-ending when you feel like you’re lacking direction. Would you mind sharing your screen so we could navigate through some resources together?”

Student: “Sure.”

Coach: “If you click on the resources button, there’s a document in there titled “Study Guide.” Let’s take a look at that.”

Student: “Yeah, I’ve seen this already. The “study guide” in the course resources is just a bulleted list of the learning objectives. I want a study guide that asks me questions where I have to search for an answer to fill in.”

Coach: “Hmm, okay. So what I’m hearing is that the study guide in this form isn’t the most valuable for you, and you’d like something a little more interactive. Is that correct?”

Student: “Yes. This just doesn’t feel like it does much for me, honestly.”

Coach: “Before we ditch this altogether, would you be open to exploring it a little more?”

Student: “Yeah, that’s okay.”

Coach: “You mentioned that this study guide doesn’t do much for you because you want something that keeps you actively engaged. How do you think you could tweak this resource to make it more suitable for you?”

Student: “Well, I’m not sure. I guess I could put these learning objectives into question form?”

Coach: “Absolutely. What else?”

Student: “I really don’t know what else I could do with it.”

Coach: “That’s okay. Here’s an idea: what if you identified the main concepts inside each learning objective, used those main topics as headings, and turned this into a note-taking template? How could that approach add value for you?”

Student: “I could definitely see that working. I never considered transforming this study guide into something else.”

6. Close with a student-owned next step

Invite the student to decide how they will use the resource after the appointment. Make the next step specific enough to act on and explore any barriers to following through.

  • “What will you work on next using this resource?”
  • “When would you like to try that?”
  • “What could make it difficult to follow through?”
  • “What will you do if the resource does not answer your question?”
Before you move on, think about:

What did the student get to do? What did their reflection help you understand? How did the conversation help them choose a useful next action?
