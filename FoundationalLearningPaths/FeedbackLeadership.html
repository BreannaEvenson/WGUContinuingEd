Embracing Feedback

The Power of 1% Improvement

Professional growth does not usually happen through one major breakthrough. More often, it happens through a series of small, intentional adjustments made over time. The idea of 1% improvement is simple: instead of expecting yourself to become dramatically better all at once, focus on making one small improvement each time you practice a skill.

In coaching, that 1% might look like asking one stronger follow-up question, allowing a few more seconds of silence before responding, narrowing an appointment to one strategy instead of several, or checking for understanding more intentionally. None of these changes may feel dramatic in a single appointment or live event, but repeated consistently, small improvements begin to change the quality of your coaching.

This approach also changes how we think about feedback. Feedback does not mean that everything you are doing needs to change. Often, feedback is simply helping you identify the next small adjustment that could make you more effective. Instead of asking, “Am I good at this or not?” a 1% improvement mindset asks, “What is one thing I can do a little better next time?”

Trying to improve too many things at once can become overwhelming. When we identify one specific behavior, practice it intentionally, and reflect on what happened, development becomes much more manageable. Once that behavior begins to feel natural, we can choose the next area of focus. Over time, those small adjustments add up.

A coach who consistently looks for their next 1% improvement becomes more intentional, adaptable, and confident.

Mindset

Growing Your Mindset

Think back over the different parts of your life where you had to learn something difficult: school, work, relationships, sports, parenting, coaching, or even learning how to handle pressure. At some point, you probably ran into something that did not come naturally to you. Maybe you struggled, made mistakes, received criticism, or watched someone else seem to “get it” faster than you did.

In those moments, most of us develop a story about what that struggle means.

Sometimes the story sounds like:

“I’m just not good at this.”

“I’ve always been bad at speaking up.”

“I’m just not a confident person.”

“This is just how I am.”

Other times, the story sounds more like:

“I’m not good at this yet.”

“I need more practice.”

“I can probably get better if I figure out what isn’t working.”

“This is uncomfortable, but discomfort is part of learning.”

Those two ways of thinking reflect the difference between a fixed mindset and a growth mindset.

Mindset

Fixed Mindset vs. Growth Mindset

A fixed mindset assumes that abilities are largely set. You either have a skill or you do not. When someone operates from that mindset, mistakes can feel threatening because they seem to reveal something permanent about who they are. Feedback can feel personal. Being corrected can feel embarrassing. Difficult tasks may become something to avoid because struggling seems like evidence that you are not capable.

A growth mindset views ability differently. It recognizes that skills can be developed through practice, feedback, reflection, and effort. Struggle is not proof that you cannot do something; it is often evidence that you are still learning how to do it. Feedback becomes information instead of a judgment.

This does not mean a growth mindset is simply “stay positive” or “believe you can do anything.” It is more practical than that. A growth mindset asks:

What can I learn from this?   What can I try differently?   What support might help me improve?

Mindset

Notice the Story You Tell Yourself

Your mindset also affects what happens after feedback. If feedback feels like a verdict, the natural response may be to defend yourself, explain why something happened, avoid the situation, or hope the issue simply goes away. If feedback is viewed as information for growth, the response becomes more productive: ask questions, choose a specific behavior to practice, seek support, try something different, and evaluate whether it worked.

Most people do not operate with a growth mindset or a fixed mindset all the time. You might have a growth mindset in one area and a fixed mindset in another. You may welcome feedback about a skill you are still learning but become defensive when the feedback touches something you believe you should already be good at.

The next time you receive feedback, pay attention to your first internal reaction. Instead of judging that reaction, notice it. Then ask yourself:

Is this thought helping me learn, or is it helping me protect myself from feeling uncomfortable?

Feedback Responses

How We Respond Matters

When we receive feedback, our response can shape what happens next just as much as the feedback itself. Two people can hear the exact same message and walk away with very different outcomes depending on how they respond. A useful way to think about feedback reactions is through four common response types: productive, defensive, clarifying, and avoidant.

Productive

A productive response is open, thoughtful, and focused on growth. The person listens to the feedback, considers what they can learn from it, and identifies what they can do differently. A productive response does not mean automatically agreeing with every piece of feedback. It means treating the feedback as useful information rather than as a personal attack.

Defensive

A defensive response happens when the first goal becomes protecting yourself rather than understanding the feedback. This can look like explaining, justifying, blaming circumstances, or immediately pointing out why the feedback is not fair or accurate. There may be legitimate context to share, but moving straight into defense can cause us to miss something useful in the feedback.

Feedback Responses

Clarify, Reflect, and Decide What Comes Next

Clarifying

A clarifying response is an important and healthy part of receiving feedback. Clarifying means asking questions so you fully understand what the other person observed and what improvement might look like. Questions such as, “Can you give me an example of where I could have slowed down?” can turn broad feedback into something specific and actionable.

Avoidant

An avoidant response happens when someone tries to move away from the feedback rather than engage with it. This might look like changing the subject, staying unusually quiet, agreeing quickly without reflection, avoiding follow-up conversations, or simply not doing anything differently afterward.

These response types are not permanent personality traits. Most people move between them depending on the situation, the topic, and sometimes who is delivering the feedback.

A strong feedback response often combines clarifying and productive behaviors: listen, ask questions, identify the key takeaway, and decide how you will apply it. That is what turns feedback from a conversation into development.
